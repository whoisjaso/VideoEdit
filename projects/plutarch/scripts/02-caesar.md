# Chapter 2: Caesar. Script v1 (immersive, same grammar as Chapter 1)

**Voices**
- **GEORGE** = the story (bm_george).
- **FABLE** = the mirror: names the belief, gives a modern example, asks one question (bm_fable).
- **Characters**, one voice each for their own spoken lines: SULLA (bm_daniel), CICERO (bm_lewis), BALBUS (bm_lewis), ARTEMIDORUS (bm_daniel), SEER (bm_lewis).
- The **Prologue** (`00-prologue.md`, voice GUIDE) opens every chapter.

**Pace:** speed 0.82, a breath between lines, a longer pause at every voice change.

**Picture cues:** the `[▸ …]` lines are for me, not spoken.

**Source:** Plutarch's *Life of Caesar*, retold in our own modern words. Plutarch paired Caesar with Alexander in his *Parallel Lives*: the two lives were meant to be read together. Doubtful stories are flagged as doubtful.

**Length:** about 3,000 words ≈ 28–30 minutes at sleep pace.

**The arc:**
- Alexander built his identity in private and lost it to flattery.
- Caesar is the opposite lesson. His identity was unbreakable: pirates, storms, a dictator's death list. But he never stopped measuring it against someone else's story.
- And at the end, he got the name, but stopped reading the warnings.

---

### Cold open: the man who wept over Alexander
[▸ Spain, a summer evening; a man's hands (point of view) turning the pages of a scroll; lamplight; the scroll shows a portrait of Alexander; then the man's face in shadow, a tear catching the light.]

**GEORGE:** Last time, you lived the life of Alexander. Tonight, you'll live the life of a man who read about him and wept.

**GEORGE:** He was already a governor, already famous in Rome, already older than Alexander had been when he conquered the world. One quiet evening in Spain, he sat reading the story of Alexander. He read for a long time. Then he put it down, and he cried.

**GEORGE:** His friends were confused. They asked him what was wrong. And he said:

**GEORGE:** *Don't you think it's worth crying over? At my age, Alexander was already king of so many nations, and I have done nothing great.*

**FABLE:** Notice what happened. A story about another man reached into him and rewrote how he saw himself. That's the power of the stories we let in. It can light a fire. It can also put you on a scoreboard you can never win. His name was Gaius Julius Caesar. Tonight, you won't watch him. You'll be him.

[▸ Title: *Plutarch's Lives*, then *Caesar*.]

---

### 1. A writer who noticed the hair
[▸ Plutarch's bust; a lamp; then a Roman street; a young man touching his hair with one finger.]

**GEORGE:** Plutarch wrote about Caesar about a hundred and fifty years after his death. As always, he cared about small things. He tells us that the great speaker Cicero suspected Caesar wanted to rule Rome alone. But then Cicero said this:

**CICERO:** When I see his hair, so carefully arranged, and watch him scratch his head with one finger, I can't believe this man would ever plan something so terrible.

**FABLE:** The mask works. A careful haircut. A light gesture. People stopped looking at what he was building and looked at how he seemed. Tonight, look past the hair. Watch the choices.

---

### 2. The death list (IMMERSE)
[▸ Rome at night; a list of names nailed to a wall; then the Sabine hills; you, feverish, moving from house to house by lamplight; soldiers' torches on the road.]

**GEORGE:** You are eighteen. Rome is ruled by Sulla, a dictator who posts lists of names. If your name is on the list, anyone can kill you.

**GEORGE:** Sulla gives you an order: divorce your wife, Cornelia, because her father was his enemy. Older, more powerful men have already obeyed orders like this.

[▸ Silence. A door, a candle, your wife's hand.]

**GEORGE:** What do you do?

**GEORGE:** You refuse. Sulla takes her dowry. He takes your position. And you have to run.

**GEORGE:** You're sick with fever, and every night you sleep in a different house. One night soldiers find you on the road. You don't fight. You talk, and you pay their captain to let you go.

**GEORGE:** When Sulla's friends beg him to spare you, he finally agrees, and warns them:

**SULLA:** There are many Mariuses in this boy.

**FABLE:** Marius was Sulla's greatest enemy. So the most dangerous man in Rome looked at a sick eighteen-year-old in hiding and saw a threat. The Observer saw it too. A boy who says no to a dictator files a note that never leaves him: *I don't bend.* Most of us bend for much less. A boss, a group chat, a friend's opinion of our girlfriend.

**FABLE:** What's one thing you've refused to give up, even when it cost you?

---

### 3. The pirates (IMMERSE)
[▸ A sunlit sea; a pirate ship, close; rough hands, ropes; then the pirates' island camp; you lying on a blanket, a wax tablet in your hand, men around a fire.]

**GEORGE:** You're twenty-five, sailing across the Aegean, when pirates take your ship. They are the most violent men on the sea. They tell you your ransom: twenty talents of silver.

**GEORGE:** You laugh at them. *You don't know who you've caught,* you say. *Ask for fifty.*

**GEORGE:** You send your companions to raise the money, and you stay behind with one friend and two servants, among men who kill for a living.

[▸ Silence. The camp at night; waves; the fire.]

**GEORGE:** Now listen to how you live for the next thirty-eight days.
- When you want to sleep, you send word telling them to be quiet.
- You join their games and exercises, as if they were your guards, not your captors.
- You write poems and speeches, and read them aloud to them. If they don't admire them, you call them uneducated barbarians to their faces.
- And again and again, laughing, you tell them that one day you'll hang them all.

**GEORGE:** They think it's charming. They laugh along.

**GEORGE:** The ransom arrives, and you're freed. You go straight to the nearest port, hire ships, sail back, and capture them. And you keep your promise.

**FABLE:** Stop on this one. Every room asks the Observer: *who are we, here?* That camp gave a loud answer: *you are a prisoner.* Chains, knives, and men who decide whether you live. And Caesar's Observer simply refused it. *No. We are Caesar. These men work for us; they just don't know it yet.*

**FABLE:** This is the job interview, turned inside out. The room tells you to sit small, and you decide who you are before the room does. That's not acting. It's evidence, built over years, strong enough to overrule a room full of knives.

**FABLE:** Where in your life does the room decide who you are? What would happen if you decided first?

---

### 4. First in a village
[▸ The Alps; a poor mountain village, smoke from a few roofs; Caesar's companions laughing on horseback; then Spain and the scroll from the cold open.]

**GEORGE:** Years later, crossing the Alps, Caesar passes a tiny village of a few poor huts. His friends joke: *Do you think, even here, they fight over who's in charge?*

**GEORGE:** Caesar answers seriously: *I would rather be first here than second in Rome.*

**GEORGE:** And it was on that same posting, in Spain, that he read the life of Alexander and wept.

**FABLE:** Put those two moments side by side. *First here, or nothing.* And *Alexander did more.* That's significance, the same need that drove Alexander: *my father will leave me nothing great to do.* But Caesar's version has a twist. He didn't just compete with the men around him. He competed with a story.

**FABLE:** We do this every night. You scroll, you see someone younger with more, and something in you sinks. Comparison borrows another person's evidence and holds it up against your own. The Observer can't win that game, because the scoreboard belongs to someone else.

**FABLE:** Whose story do you measure yourself against? Did you choose them, or did your feed?

---

### 5. War as medicine
[▸ Gaul: forests, rain, a long marching column; a thin man on a horse dictating to two scribes riding beside him; then a carriage at night, the man asleep inside, the road moving.]

**GEORGE:** Then came Gaul. For nearly ten years Caesar fought there. Plutarch counts more than eight hundred towns taken and three hundred peoples conquered.

**GEORGE:** But Plutarch is more interested in the man than the numbers. Caesar was thin, with soft pale skin. He suffered headaches, and he had seizures. He could have used his body as his excuse.

**GEORGE:** He did the opposite. He made war his medicine. Long marches. Plain food. Sleeping outdoors. He slept in carriages while travelling, so no hour was wasted. On horseback, he dictated letters to two writers at once.

**GEORGE:** And his soldiers loved him for it, because whatever he asked of them, they watched him do first.

**FABLE:** Identity is built in exactly these hours. Nobody claps for the march in the rain. Nobody films you eating plain food when you could have had anything. But the Observer is there for every step, writing: *we don't use excuses.* And soldiers, like children, don't follow what you say. They follow what they see you do.

**FABLE:** What weakness of yours could become your training, instead of your excuse?

---

### 6. The surrender
[▸ Alesia: the double ring of Roman walls; a gate opening; a rider in shining armour on a decorated horse, circling a seated Roman; then the rider kneeling, armour on the ground.]

**GEORGE:** At Alesia, the greatest Gallic leader, Vercingetorix, finally loses. And Plutarch describes his surrender like a scene from a play.

**GEORGE:** He puts on his most beautiful armour. He decorates his horse. He rides out through the gate, and circles once around Caesar, who is sitting in judgment. Then he jumps down, throws off his armour, and sits at Caesar's feet. He doesn't say a word.

**FABLE:** Even in defeat, he chose who he would be. He arrived as a king and laid the king down himself. No one could take that from him, because he gave it. There's dignity in deciding your own ending.

---

### 7. The river (IMMERSE)
[▸ Night, a small river; the bridge; torches on the water; you on horseback at the edge, the army stopped behind you; your friends' faces; then hooves on the bridge.]

**GEORGE:** You're fifty years old. Rome has ordered you to give up your army and come home alone. Your enemies are waiting there to put you on trial and end your career.

**GEORGE:** Ahead of you is a small river called the Rubicon. It marks the border. If you cross it with soldiers, it's war. Roman against Roman. There's no going back.

**GEORGE:** You stop at the edge. You don't speak. You think about everything this will cost: the suffering for all of Italy, and what people will say about you a thousand years from now. You change your mind. Then you change it back. You talk it through with your friends.

[▸ Silence. The river, running in the dark.]

**GEORGE:** What do you do?

**GEORGE:** Plutarch says that finally, with a kind of passion, as if throwing yourself out of your calculations and into the future, you say the words men say before a desperate venture: *Let the die be cast.*

**GEORGE:** And you cross.

**FABLE:** Look at the two halves of that moment. First, the calculating: all the futures, all the opinions, all the judgment to come. That's where anxiety lives. Then, a single act, in the present, and the whole future changed.

**FABLE:** Most of us live on the bank of the river. We think it over again, and again, and call it being careful. But nothing becomes real until you cross. The Observer doesn't record what you considered. It records what you did.

**FABLE:** What's your Rubicon? What are you still standing beside, thinking about?

---

### 8. The storm (IMMERSE)
[▸ A dark harbour; you in a slave's cloak, climbing into a small boat; the sea rising; waves over the bow; the frightened captain at the steering oar; your hand on his.]

**GEORGE:** Now the civil war is real. You need your army across the sea, and it's late. So one night, you disguise yourself as a slave and climb into a small boat to go and fetch them yourself.

**GEORGE:** At the mouth of the river, a storm hits. Waves crash over the bow. The captain shouts to turn back.

**GEORGE:** You stand up, pull off your hood, and take his hand. *Don't be afraid,* you tell him. *You're carrying Caesar, and Caesar's luck.*

**GEORGE:** The sailors forget the storm. They row as hard as men can row.

**GEORGE:** And still the sea is too strong. In the end, you have to turn back.

**FABLE:** Both halves of this matter. Identity moves people. One sentence turned frightened sailors into heroes. That's what happens when you truly know who you are: other people borrow your belief.

**FABLE:** But the sea doesn't care who you are. This is the first crack, where identity can start becoming ego: when you begin to believe the world itself has to obey your story. Confidence moves men. It doesn't move oceans.

---

### 9. The ring
[▸ Egypt: a palace by the sea; a man holding out a basket covered with cloth; Caesar turning away; then a ring on an open palm: a lion holding a sword; tears.]

**GEORGE:** Caesar defeats his great rival, Pompey, at Pharsalus. Pompey flees to Egypt, where the young king's advisers murder him, hoping to please the winner.

**GEORGE:** When Caesar arrives, they bring him Pompey's head. Caesar turns away from the man who holds it, as if he were cursed. Then they hand him Pompey's ring, carved with a lion holding a sword.

**GEORGE:** And Caesar weeps.

**GEORGE:** He pardons many of Pompey's friends, including a young man named Brutus. Remember his name.

**FABLE:** He could have celebrated. His enemy was gone, and he didn't even have to do it. Instead, he wept for a man he'd fought for years. Power with restraint: like Alexander at the tent of Darius. When you're secure in who you are, your enemy's fall doesn't feel like your victory. It feels like a loss of something human.

---

### 10. Three words
[▸ A wax tablet; a stylus writing three words; a messenger riding; a Roman crowd hearing them read aloud.]

**GEORGE:** In Asia, Caesar wins a war so quickly that he reports it to a friend in Rome in three words: *Veni, vidi, vici.* I came, I saw, I conquered.

**GEORGE:** And in Africa, his most stubborn enemy, Cato, chooses to take his own life rather than be pardoned. When Caesar hears, he says: *Cato, I begrudge you your death, because you begrudged me the chance to save your life.*

**FABLE:** Listen to that carefully. Even his mercy had a little ego in it. He wanted to be the man who forgave. Cato refused to give him that. Who in your life can't be impressed by you, not even by your kindness? Notice how you feel about them.

---

### 11. Remember who you are
[▸ Rome: the Forum; gold statues; a man seated on a high chair; senators approaching in a long line; one man leaning close to his ear.]

**GEORGE:** Back in Rome, the honours pour in. Statues. Titles. Then, dictator for life. Plutarch calls it what it was: a tyranny.

**GEORGE:** One day the whole Senate comes to give Caesar new honours. And he stays in his seat. Some say he started to stand, but a friend held him down and whispered:

**BALBUS:** Remember that you are Caesar.

**FABLE:** There it is. Not *remember what you've done.* *Remember what you are.* That's how the flatterers work. They don't lie to you about your actions. They talk to your mask. They make the title feel like the person.

**FABLE:** It happens in small rooms too. The friend who says you're always right. The followers who agree before you finish. The Observer used to ask, *what did we do today?* Now it hears, *remember who you are,* and stops checking.

---

### 12. The crown
[▸ The Lupercalia festival: runners in the street; Antony with a gold diadem wrapped in laurel; the crowd, half silent; Caesar's hand pushing it away; the roar.]

**GEORGE:** At a festival, Mark Antony runs up to Caesar and holds out a crown, a king's diadem, wrapped in laurel.

**GEORGE:** A few people clap. Not many. Caesar pushes it away, and the whole crowd roars.

**GEORGE:** Antony offers it again. Again a few. Caesar refuses it again, and again the whole crowd cheers.

**GEORGE:** Caesar stands and orders the crown taken to the temple of Jupiter. Soon after, crowns appear on his statues. When two officials pull them down, Caesar removes them from office.

**FABLE:** The people told him clearly what they wanted: not a king. He heard the cheer, put the crown down, and then punished the men who took the crowns off his statues. When the ego wants something the people won't give, it doesn't stop wanting it. It just stops listening to them.

---

### 13. A sudden one
[▸ A dinner by lamplight; Caesar signing letters at the table while the others talk; a cup raised; his eyes not leaving the page.]

**GEORGE:** His friends warn him to keep a bodyguard. He dismisses it. *It's better,* he says, *to die once than to live always expecting it.*

**GEORGE:** A seer has warned him of great danger on a day in March, the Ides.

**GEORGE:** The night before the Ides, he's at dinner, signing letters while the others talk. Someone asks: *what's the best kind of death?* Before anyone can answer, Caesar looks up and says: *A sudden one.*

**FABLE:** Fearless, yes. But listen to what's underneath. *I'm too big to be careful.* Courage and arrogance can sound exactly the same. The difference is whether you're still listening.

---

### 14. The note (IMMERSE)
[▸ Dawn; a bedroom; a woman waking in terror; doors swinging open by themselves; then the street, a crowd pressing in; a hand pushing a small folded note into yours; the steps of the Senate; your fingers closed around the paper.]

**GEORGE:** The night before, your wife Calpurnia wakes up crying. She dreamed she was holding you in her arms, murdered. In the morning she begs you not to go to the Senate today. You've never seen her like this.

**GEORGE:** You almost listen. You decide to send someone to postpone the meeting. Then a friend arrives, laughing. *Will you wait for your wife to have better dreams?* He takes your hand and leads you out the door.

**GEORGE:** The streets are crowded. A Greek teacher named Artemidorus pushes through the crowd. He knows about the plot. He's written it all down. He presses the note into your hand.

**ARTEMIDORUS:** Read this, Caesar. Alone, and quickly. It concerns you.

**GEORGE:** You take it. You try to read it, again and again, but people keep stopping you. Greeting you. Asking for things. You walk on, holding it in your hand.

[▸ Silence. The folded paper in your fingers.]

**GEORGE:** What do you do?

**GEORGE:** On the way, you pass the seer who warned you. You smile and say: *The Ides of March have come.*

**SEER:** Yes, Caesar. They have come. But they have not yet gone.

**GEORGE:** And you walk inside, the note still in your hand. Unread.

**FABLE:** Everything he needed was in his own hand. His wife's warning. The seer's warning. A message that would have saved him. He was too busy being Caesar to read it.

**FABLE:** That's the deepest cost of ego. Not that it makes you cruel. That it makes you deaf. The crowd, the greetings, the requests: that's the applause. And the truth is a small folded note you never open.

**FABLE:** What warning are you holding in your hand right now, unread?

---

### 15. The statue
[▸ The Senate house; a marble statue of Pompey; men gathering close; a toga pulled; the flash of a blade; then a face (Brutus) and a hand drawing a toga over a head. Slow, then still.]

**GEORGE:** Inside, the conspirators gather close, pretending to plead with him. One pulls his toga down from his neck. That's the signal. The first blade strikes.

**GEORGE:** Caesar fights back. He turns this way and that, but everywhere he turns, there's another blade.

**GEORGE:** Then he sees Brutus, the young man he pardoned, with his dagger drawn. And Plutarch says Caesar stopped fighting. He pulled his toga over his face, and sank down at the foot of a statue. The statue of Pompey.

[▸ Long silence.]

**GEORGE:** He had twenty-three wounds. It was as if Pompey himself were presiding over his revenge.

**FABLE:** He covered his face. When the man he had spared raised a blade against him, the fight left him. Not the blade. The betrayal.

---

### 16. Only the name
[▸ The Forum at night; a funeral fire; the crowd; then a comet over Rome; then quiet: an empty seat.]

**GEORGE:** Rome erupted. At his funeral the crowd built his pyre in the Forum and ran through the streets with torches, hunting his killers. Within a few years, not one of his murderers was left. Plutarch tells of a great comet that blazed for seven nights, and of a ghost that visited Brutus before his final battle.

**GEORGE:** And then Plutarch gives his verdict. Caesar had chased power all his life, through enormous danger. And when he finally had it, all he got from it was the name, and the envy that came with it.

---

### 17. Your turn
[▸ The gold dot travels back through every moment: the list on the wall, the pirates' fire, the scroll of Alexander, the river, the storm, the ring, the crown, the unread note. Then darkness and a single candle.]

**GEORGE:** Alexander and Caesar. Plutarch set their lives side by side so you would read them together. One built himself in private and lost himself to flattery. The other was so certain of who he was that pirates couldn't shake it, a dictator couldn't bend it, and a storm couldn't scare it. And still, at the very end, he stopped listening.

**FABLE:** So here's what you lived tonight.
- **Decide who you are before the room does.** In the pirates' camp, the room said prisoner. He said Caesar. Your identity can overrule your environment, if you've built the evidence.
- **Don't let someone else's story become your scoreboard.** He wept over Alexander's life instead of living his own. The feed will always show you someone further ahead.
- **Cross your river.** Calculation lives in the future. Change only happens in the present, in one act.
- **Keep reading the notes.** Ego doesn't always make you cruel. Sometimes it just makes you deaf. The day you're too important to listen is the day you're most in danger.

**FABLE:** So, as you drift off, ask the Observer one quiet question. *Is there a warning I'm holding that I haven't read?* Then sleep. Tomorrow, open it.

**GEORGE:** That was the life of Caesar. Sleep well.
