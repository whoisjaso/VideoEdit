# What the script claims, and what stands behind it

Rule from the brief: a claim about the brain is one the research supports; a model is called a model; no number without a named study.
Only the Nosek 2007 finding was re-checked against the paper during this session. The rest are cited from memory of well-known papers; confirm the page references before quoting them in a post or a reply.

| Line in the film | Status | Source |
|---|---|---|
| "Researchers have a name for this: cultivation." Heavy viewers come to expect the world the screen shows. | Supported. Effects are small but consistent across studies. | Gerbner & Gross (1976), "Living with television: the violence profile," *Journal of Communication* 26(2). On screen as "Gerbner & Gross, 1976". |
| "There's a test for it, the implicit association test, millions of people have taken it." | Supported. | Greenwald, McGhee & Schwartz (1998), *JPSP* 74(6). Project Implicit has collected millions of sessions. On screen as "Greenwald, McGhee & Schwartz, 1998". |
| "Most people's brains pair the same faces with the same words faster than the others." | Supported for test-takers overall. | Nosek et al. (2007), "Pervasiveness and correlates of implicit attitudes and stereotypes," *European Review of Social Psychology* 18(1). |
| "And it shows up in people of every race." | Supported as worded. **The brief's original wording was not**: Nosek et al. report that Black participants show no implicit preference on average. Individuals of every race show the pairing; groups do not show it equally. Line changed (REPORT.md §3). | Same paper (checked this session). |
| The officer's split-second "gangster means gun." | Supported as a lab finding (faster "shoot" decisions for armed Black targets). The film shows a scene, not a statistic. | Correll, Park, Judd & Wittenbrink (2002), "The police officer's dilemma," *JPSP* 83(6). |
| "Each one is proving the other one's movie right." | Supported as a mechanism (expectations change the other person's behaviour). | Word, Zanna & Cooper (1974), *Journal of Experimental Social Psychology* 10(2). |
| "That's what we are… repetition becomes identity." | A model. Related evidence: stereotype threat. The film does not claim a measured effect size. | Steele & Aronson (1995), *JPSP* 69(5). |
| Code-switching "costs something. Every time." | Supported by survey and interview work; "every time" is rhetoric. | McCluney et al. (2019), "The costs of code-switching," *Harvard Business Review*. |
| "The script runs in about a quarter of a second. The observer catches it in the half-second after." | **A model, said as one.** Narration now opens with "Here's a model of it." and the graphic is labelled "a model, not a measurement". Loosely after Libet's timing work, which is itself contested. | Libet et al. (1983), *Brain* 106. |
| "Your brain has one job: keep you alive." | Framing, not a finding. Left as written. | n/a |
| "ten thousand hours of screens" | **Cut.** No named study behind the number. Now "thousands of hours". | n/a |

Known limit worth having ready for the comments: the IAT measures pairing speed. It is a weak predictor of any one person's behaviour (Oswald et al., 2013, *JPSP* 105(2)). The script only claims the pairing, which is why it holds.
